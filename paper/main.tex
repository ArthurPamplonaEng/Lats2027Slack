\begin{abstract}
Phase-swept slack sensors report the timing margin of a path as a count of clock-manager phase
steps, and silicon lifecycle management (SLM) acts on that count, yet whether a zero reading marks
the functional failure of the path is rarely measured. We drive a delay chain on a 16\,nm Artix
UltraScale+ FPGA through failure by lowering the core voltage; each of its \vTapN{} taps carries
the sensor and a functional checker on the same capture flip-flop. On \vTapNoGN{} failing taps in
two placements, the first error came between \vWorstLatePs\,ps before and \vTapNoGMaxPsAbs\,ps after
the reading reached zero, and \vTapNoGLateSure{} taps failed while still reading positive. This
offset is the sensor's own sampling skew: static timing predicts it per tap ($r=\vFitR$,
\vFitRms\,ps rms) once the clock uncertainty and pessimism charged to the sensor's cross-clock path
are removed. A static phase on the system clock turns it into a designed guard band: a 250\,ps
setting lowered every reading by \vGuardPtPs\,ps without moving the failure voltage, after which
every tap read zero before failing. On a 28\,nm Artix-7, a \vRunDurH\,h burn-in at 1.4\,V and
125\,\textdegree C took \vDpsHiAbs, \vDpsMidAbs{} and \vDpsLoAbs\,ps of slack from a 16-bit adder
path at 1.1, 1.0 and 0.9\,V, which a single supply shift of \vShiftmV\,mV accounts for. A zero
reading is thus a failure warning only once its offset is known; static timing bounds it and one
voltage sweep measures it.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

On-chip monitors are moving from characterization chips into products, where SLM software reads
them to set adaptive voltage scaling (AVS), raise margin alarms and estimate remaining
life~\cite{kashyap21,niemann20,vargas25}. A timing-margin sensor serves those loops only if its
reading means what they assume: that a zero reading marks the operating point at which the
monitored path starts to fail. In ASICs, failure prediction from a guard band ahead of the capture
edge~\cite{agarwal07}, error-prediction flip-flops whose guard band adapts to
aging~\cite{martins11}, and error detection at the capture flip-flop~\cite{razor04} are
established. On FPGAs, aging sensors based on phase-shifted sampling have been validated under
stress~\cite{valdes13,sensible17}; clock-sweep self-measurement of path delays goes back to Wong
\textit{et al.}~\cite{wong09} and was used to track degradation~\cite{stott10}; online slack
measurement with phase-shifted shadow registers has driven voltage and frequency
scaling~\cite{levine14}; and online delay monitors now reach every timing path, with an average
error of 1.5\,\%~\cite{jiang22}. These works track degradation or set the operating point from the
reading; to our knowledge, none measures, path by path, how far a zero reading lies from the first
functional error of the path being watched.

A dynamic phase shift added to the sensor of~\cite{vargas24} lets it measure the slack of a path
continuously, without recalibration~\cite{sbcci25}. The question SLM needs answered remains open:
\emph{how far from failure is a path whose reading is zero?} The obstacle is practical: a path with
enough slack to be safe at the nominal point does not fail within the voltage range of a board, and
one path gives one failure point.

This paper answers the question with measurements on two FPGA generations:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A multi-endpoint test structure (Section~\ref{sec:struct}): taps of graded length on one
ripple chain, each with the sensor and a functional checker on the same capture flip-flop, so one
core-voltage sweep moves the failure boundary across many paths under the same conditions.
\item The answer on 16\,nm (Section~\ref{sec:res16}): the zero reading and the first error coincide
to within an offset of $-$\vWorstLatePs{} to $+$\vTapNoGMaxPsAbs\,ps that belongs to the sensor's
implementation, not to the path. Static timing analysis (STA) predicts it per tap, also on a second
placement, and a static clock phase moves it by design without touching the failure voltage, so
the guard band an SLM loop must add can be bounded at design time and checked with one shmoo.
\item The margin a burn-in consumes, read through the sensor on 28\,nm (Section~\ref{sec:res28}):
the steps lost grow from \vDslackHiAbs{} at 1.1\,V to \vDslackLoAbs{} at 0.9\,V, yet one supply shift
of \vShiftmV\,mV reproduces all three corners, as a uniform threshold-voltage increase would; the
temperature coefficient of the reading changes sign near 1.0\,V. A \vUsDurH\,h stress of two
16\,nm boards at nominal voltage, by contrast, moved their readings by less than one step.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Sensor and Reading}\label{sec:sensor}

The sensor~\cite{sbcci25} samples the monitored path with two flip-flops (Fig.~\ref{fig:struct}):
FF2 on the system clock and FF1 on a clock whose phase the clock manager (MMCM) shifts at run
time. FF3 latches their XOR on a catcher clock a fixed fraction of the period later. A controller
moves the FF1 clock earlier one fine step at a time, $\delta\phi = 1/(56 f_\mathrm{VCO})$~\cite{ug572}
(17.857\,ps at $f_\mathrm{VCO}=1$\,GHz), and the reading $r$ is the number of steps before the first
alarm. The path toggles every cycle, so the alarm is deterministic up to jitter.

\textbf{What a reading means.} Let $s_1$ and $s_2$ be the setup slacks of the path at FF1 (at phase
step 0) and at FF2. FF1 sees the previous value, and the alarm fires, once $r\,\delta\phi > s_1$, so
$r=\lceil s_1/\delta\phi\rceil$; the functional capture fails when $s_2<0$. The two slacks differ by
an implementation offset $\Delta=s_2-s_1$: the skew between the two clock trees at FF1 and FF2, the
routing from the path to each flip-flop, and their setup times. If $\Delta>0$ the reading reaches
zero while FF2 still captures correctly, a warning margin of $\Delta$; if $\Delta<0$ the sensor is
late, and the path fails while it still reads $|\Delta|/\delta\phi$ steps. In voltage the margin is
$\Delta/|\partial d/\partial\Vcc|$, with $d$ the path delay.

\textbf{After failure.} Once a path fails ($T<d<2T$), FF1 and FF2 capture the same stale value at
step 0, and the next alarm appears only near $r=(2T-d)/\delta\phi$: beyond the sweep (no alarm) or as
a large, aliased reading. The same blindness applies to one edge: when the slower transition fails
at both flip-flops, the reading reflects the other edge. A reading alone cannot tell a failing path
from a safe one; a functional checker can.

\section{Multi-Endpoint Test Structure}\label{sec:struct}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\resizebox{\columnwidth}{!}{\input{figures/fig_struct.tex}}
\caption{Test structure (16\,nm). A ripple chain whose carry toggles every cycle feeds 23 taps
($S_j=24$, 48, and 70 to 150 in steps of 4); each tap has the sensor of~\cite{sbcci25} and a checker
that compares the capture flip-flop FF2 with a replica $t$ of the stimulus. One phase sweep reads
every tap; the FPGA keeps 1\,s statistics per tap.}
\label{fig:struct}
\end{figure}

The chain has \vChainStages{} stages, one LUT3 (carry majority) per stage. Operand $a$ alternates
between 0x55..55 and 0x55..56 and $b=$\,0xAA..AA, so every carry toggles each cycle and both edges
ripple through the whole chain.
Tap $j$ takes the sum bit of stage $S_j$ through its own LUT, so all taps load the chain alike. Two
control taps never fail; the other \vTapNlong{} are four stages apart so that the 10\,ns period is
crossed inside the set over the whole voltage range. Each tap feeds a sensor and a checker
$e_j=\mathrm{FF2}_j\oplus t$, which is 0 in every cycle while the tap meets timing; the checker is a
short single-cycle path, so an error is the monitored path's. A sweep engine moves the FF1 clock
until every tap has alarmed or \vMaxSteps{} steps (inside the catcher window) have passed, returns
the phase to its origin and repeats, about \vTapSweepsPerS{} times per second. For every 1\,s window
and tap the FPGA keeps the number of sweeps with an alarm, the mean, minimum and maximum reading,
and the number of cycles with $e_j=1$; a virtual I/O core clocked by the board oscillator exposes
them to the JTAG.

\textbf{Implementation.} The design targets xcau15p-ffvb676-1-i (Vivado 2025.2). Chain, sensors and
checkers occupy one exclusive region; placement A and placement B put that region in two clock
regions of the die, which gives the same logic two different routings. Each tap path has a
reachable max-delay target (0.6\,ns + 0.15\,ns per stage) instead of the impossible 10\,ns
requirement. Two details matter for the sensor. First, Vivado models MMCM output phases as clock
latency, so by default it timed FF2$\rightarrow$FF3 against the \emph{next} catcher edge and padded
the route by 5\,ns to meet a spurious hold requirement, which would have shrunk the observation
window to about 3.8\,ns; a setup multicycle of 0 restores the same-edge capture (0.56\,ns of
routing). Second, the MMCM forbids a static phase on the output with fine phase shift, so the
sensor carries no designed guard band: $\Delta$ is whatever the implementation gives. STA reports
it per tap as the difference of the FF2 and FF1 setup slacks, \vStaDeltaLo{} to \vStaDeltaHi\,ns in
the slow corner, always positive.

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}

\textbf{16\,nm.} The structure runs on an RV02A board (Artix UltraScale+ xcau15p, nominal
$\Vcc=0.85$\,V; board~2 of Section~\ref{sec:res16bound}) whose core rail comes from a TPS65400
regulator that an STM32 sets in 10\,mV steps; $\Vcc$ and the die temperature are read from the SYSMON through the JTAG. Each shmoo steps
$\Vcc$ from \vTapVhi{} down to \vTapVlo\,V and back (2\,s to settle, then 6 to 8 windows of 1\,s,
i.e.\ up to $8\times10^{8}$ checked cycles per point; about 8\,min per shmoo), always above the
0.72\,V operating point of the low-voltage grade of the family~\cite{ds931} and below its 1.0\,V
absolute maximum. The board sat at room temperature; its SYSMON read about \vTapDie\,\textdegree C,
a known offset of this board's sensor that we report but do not use.

\textbf{28\,nm.} A Nexys~4~DDR board (Artix-7 xc7a100t, nominal 1.0\,V) runs the same sensor, with
the same 17.857\,ps step, on the most significant sum bit of a 16-bit ripple-carry adder whose
inputs toggle every cycle. An IT6502D supply feeds the core rail and is trimmed every second against
the XADC reading of $\Vcc$; the board sits in an oven under PID control~\cite{sbcci26}. The burn-in
(Run~1) lasted \vRunDurH\,h at $\Vcc=\vRunV$\,V ($\pm$\vRunVsdmV\,mV) and a die temperature of
$\vRunT\pm\vRunTsd$\,\textdegree C (oven air \vRunOven\,\textdegree C), with the sensor read every
second (\vRunN{} samples) and never recalibrated; it read $\vRunSlack\pm\vRunSlackSd$ steps
(\vRunSlackNs\,ns of slack). At about \vPreT\,\textdegree C, corners at 1.10, 1.00 and 0.90\,V were
taken before the burn-in (single readings) and right after it, in a logged run that also stepped
$\Vcc$ from \vSweepHi{} to \vSweepLo\,V; cold corners at \vColdT\,\textdegree C followed. Both stresses
are deliberate overstress (1.4\,V exceeds the absolute-maximum core voltage of the
Artix-7~\cite{ds181}), so the rates reported here do not transfer to product
burn-in~\cite{jesd22a108}.

\section{Results}

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{16\,nm}{16 nm}: Zero Reading Versus First Error}\label{sec:res16}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig_tap_shmoo}
\caption{16\,nm, one downward sweep. Top: reading of the taps whose failure boundary falls inside
the sweep, in placement A (solid) and in the same routed design with the 250\,ps guard of
Section~\ref{sec:guard} (dashed); a point is drawn while the tap's checker saw no error. Bottom:
error rate of the failing points of placement A (fraction of cycles in which FF2 held the wrong
value).}
\label{fig:shmoo}
\end{figure}

Fig.~\ref{fig:shmoo} shows one downward voltage sweep. Near its failure boundary each tap's reading
falls almost linearly with $\Vcc$, at \vTapKlo{} to \vTapKhi{} steps per mV
(\vTapKloPs--\vTapKhiPs\,ps of slack per mV), and within one 10\,mV step the checker goes from no
error in up to $8\times10^{8}$ cycles to errors. Taps shorter than the boundary never erred, nor
did the control taps at any voltage. Longer taps erred in every cycle up to a delay of two periods,
beyond which the period-two stimulus makes a capture two cycles late look correct (the longest taps
at the lowest voltages); all showed either no alarm or the aliased reading of
Section~\ref{sec:sensor}. Error rates of exactly 0.5 between the two regimes show the two edges
failing at different voltages.

For each failing tap and voltage sweep we fit a straight line to the three error-free, positive
readings closest to the boundary, extrapolate it to the failure threshold and call $m$ the negated
result, in ps: $m>0$ means the reading had reached zero before the first error. The threshold lies between the first failing and the last
passing voltage; the bounds of $m$ that these two voltages give are the error bars of
Fig.~\ref{fig:offset}. As the best estimate we take the first failing voltage when its error rate is
below 0.5, since then only the jitter tail of one edge fails there, and the middle of the interval
otherwise. Over the \vTapNoGSweepsMin{} to \vTapNoGSweepsMax{} voltage sweeps of each tap (down and
up, repeated shmoos), $m$ varied by at most \vTapNoGSdMax{} step (standard deviation), with no
hysteresis; Table~\ref{tab:taps} lists the means.

Over the \vTapNoGN{} failing taps of the two placements, $m$ ranged from \vTapNoGMinSt{} to
\vTapNoGMaxSt{} steps (\vTapNoGMinPs{} to \vTapNoGMaxPs\,ps, \vTapNoGMinmV{} to \vTapNoGMaxmV\,mV).
One tap read zero at \vZeroPtsMax{} voltage points before failing, but \vTapNoGLateSure{} of the
\vTapNoGN{} failed while still reading positive even at the favorable end of their interval, the
latest one \vWorstLateSt{} steps above zero at the best estimate. The zero reading is thus a close but not a conservative predictor: as built, the sensor can be late by
about \vWorstLatePs\,ps, or \vWorstLatemV\,mV of core voltage.

\subsection{What Sets the Offset}\label{sec:offset}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig_tap_offset}
\caption{Measured margin $m$ at failure versus the offset $\Dst$ predicted by STA without the
uncertainty and pessimism terms (slow corner). Filled: the independent failing taps of the two
placements; hollow: the same routed taps with a guard band ($\Dst$ from the STA of the guarded

bars span the 10\,mV interval in which errors start. Below zero the path fails while the sensor still

\label{fig:offset}

The tap-to-tap spread follows the implementation. STA charges the FF1 path, which crosses from the
system clock into the phase-swept clock, \vStaDU\,ps more clock uncertainty and \vStaDCPR\,ps less
clock-pessimism removal than the same-clock FF2 path; both are worst-case analysis margins, not
delays of a typical die. Removing both
from the slack difference gives $\Dst$, computed for each placement from its own routed design with
no fitted parameter. Over the \vFitN{} independent taps (Fig.~\ref{fig:offset}, filled symbols, and
Table~\ref{tab:taps}), $\Dst$ predicts the measured margin with \vFitRms\,ps rms residual, against
\vFitNullRms\,ps for the best constant offset ($r=\vFitR$, $p=\vFitP$; Spearman $\rho=\vFitRho$;
fitted slope \vFitSlope); placement B, measured after the correction had been identified on A,
follows it with \vFitRmsB\,ps rms. $\Dst$ falls inside the measured interval for \vFitInside{} taps
and misses the others by at most \vFitMissMax\,ps: the residual is comparable to the 10\,mV voltage
grid, whose interval spans $\pm$\vFitHalfW\,ps on average. It shows no trend with the failure
voltage over \vTapVfLo--\vTapVfHi\,V (correlation \vFitResVfR), so the offset does not depend
strongly on the voltage at which the path fails. The raw STA slack difference would have overstated
the margins by \vFitRawBiasAbs\,ps on average, predicting a safe sensor where most taps were late.

\subsection{A Designed Guard Band}\label{sec:guard}

The MMCM accepts a static phase on the outputs without fine phase shift, in settings of one eighth
of the VCO period (125\,ps here). Delaying the system clock (launch registers, FF2 and checkers) by
9\textdegree{} (250\,ps) makes FF1 sample that much earlier at step 0 without touching the monitored
path. We applied it to the routed designs of both placements, changing nothing but two MMCM
attributes (A$'$, B$'$; the catcher keeps its distance to FF2). At the \vGuardPtN{} tap--voltage
points measured with and without it, the reading dropped by \vGuardPtSt$\pm$\vGuardPtSd{} steps
(\vGuardPtPs\,ps; designed \vGuardDesignSt), every tap first failed at the same 10\,mV step as
before, and all \vGuardPairs{} failing taps then read zero before their first error: by
\vTapGMinSt{} to \vTapGMaxSt{} steps (\vTapGMinPs{} to \vTapGMaxPs\,ps) at the best estimate, and
\vTapGEarly{} of them even at the unfavorable end of their interval. A single setting (4.5\textdegree,
B$''$) lowered the readings of B by \vGuardHPtSt$\pm$\vGuardHPtSd{} steps (\vGuardHPtPs\,ps) instead
of \vGuardHDesignSt, equally on every tap, so the phase settings of the MMCM are not evenly spaced;
with it, all \vTapGhN{} failing taps of B also read zero first, by \vTapGhMinPs{} to
\vTapGhMaxPs\,ps (Table~\ref{tab:taps}). The guard is thus a design parameter whose realized value
must be measured once, as the reading change of any tap with and without it.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{28\,nm}{28 nm}: The Margin a Burn-In Consumes}\label{sec:res28}

Fig.~\ref{fig:run1} compares the corners at \vPreT\,\textdegree C. The burn-in lowered the reading by
\vDslackHiAbs, \vDslackMidAbs{} and \vDslackLoAbs{} steps ($\pm1$) at 1.10, 1.00 and 0.90\,V, that is
\vDpsHiAbs, \vDpsMidAbs{} and \vDpsLoAbs\,ps of slack: the loss grows as the voltage falls. One
parameter accounts for all three. Moved by \vShiftmV$\pm$\vShiftErrmV\,mV towards lower voltage, the
post-burn-in curve passes through the three pre-burn-in readings within \vShiftResMax{} step (rms
\vShiftResRms), whereas the best constant slack offset leaves up to \vOffsetResMax{} steps of
residual at the extreme corners. After the burn-in the path thus needs \vShiftmV\,mV more supply for
the same slack at every corner, as a uniform threshold-voltage increase from bias-temperature
instability would cause to first order, rather than a fixed added delay. For an SLM loop this is the
quantity of interest: the voltage at which the monitored path reads zero rose by \vShiftmV\,mV. If
the sensor's offset did not age, which we did not test, the failure voltage of the path rose by the
same amount.

Cooling from about 104 to \vColdT\,\textdegree C raised the reading by \vHotMinusColdHiAbs{} steps at
1.10\,V, left it within one step at 1.00\,V and \emph{lowered} it by
\vHotMinusColdLoAbs{} steps at 0.90\,V; the 0.90\,V plateau of the logged run, whose die drifted over
7\,\textdegree C, shows the same sign. The path is slowest hot above about 1.0\,V and slowest cold
below it (temperature inversion), so a ``maximum temperature'' corner is meaningful only above the
crossover, and a low-voltage aging corner must be measured cold.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}\label{sec:disc}

\textbf{For SLM.} A loop that treats $r=0$ as the failure point of the monitored path is off by
the sensor's implementation offset, here up to \vWorstLatePs\,ps (\vWorstLateSt{} steps, about
\vWorstLatemV\,mV of core voltage) on the unsafe side. The offset is a design-time quantity: STA
gives it per path once the analysis-only margins of the cross-clock FF1 path are removed, and a
static phase on the system clock turns it into a deliberate guard band, after which the reading is
a calibrated, conservative margin. Every step of guard is margin taken from AVS, so its size is a
trade-off: sized from STA alone, to cover the most negative $\Dst$ (\vDstMinAbs\,ps late) plus twice
the model's rms error, it would be \vGuardSizedPs\,ps (\vGuardSizedSet{} settings), where one setting
sufficed for the measured margins of B. A tapped chain closes that gap cheaply: one shmoo of about 8\,min measures
the offset of every tap. The 28\,nm result then says what aging does to the margin: a burn-in that
cost \vDslackLoAbs{} steps at 0.9\,V moved the zero-reading voltage by \vShiftmV\,mV at every
corner.

\textbf{Limitations.} The validation used one board per technology, so its results are within-device.
The 16\,nm validation was done at room temperature only: the offset is a skew and routing property,
but clock-tree delays scale with temperature, so its temperature dependence remains to be measured.
Whether the offset itself drifts under stress was not tested either. The 28\,nm adder path was not
driven to failure: at 0.90\,V and 104\,\textdegree C it still had \vPostLo{} steps (\vPostNsLo\,ns) of slack,
and its pre-burn-in corners are single readings ($\pm1$ step). The 16\,nm stress bound is limited by
the step size: when the transition sits away from a step boundary, the reading returns the same
integer in every sweep (the zero spread of some entries in Table~\ref{tab:us}); statistical
readout~\cite{wong09} or a faster VCO would lower it. RTL, constraints, bench scripts and raw logs
are available from the authors.

\section{Conclusion}

A zero slack reading is close to, but not exactly at, the failure of the monitored path: on 16\,nm
the first functional error came between \vWorstLatePs\,ps before and \vTapNoGMaxPsAbs\,ps after the
reading reached zero, an offset set by the sensor's own sampling skew. Static timing predicts that
offset per path once its cross-clock analysis margins are removed, on two placements, and a static
clock phase moves it by design, in settings of about 125\,ps, without touching the failure voltage,
which makes the reading a calibrated, conservative margin. Read that way, a 28\,nm burn-in at 1.4\,V and
125\,\textdegree C moved the zero-reading voltage of an adder path by \vShiftmV\,mV, uniformly across
the voltage range. Next steps are the temperature dependence and the aging of the offset, several
boards, and the tapped structure under stress, to watch the failure voltage itself move.
